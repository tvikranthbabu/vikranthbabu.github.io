Selected work / eBay, 2025

AI-personalised seller messaging

Asked by
The Selling Product team
My role
Science PRD, system design and evaluation, hands-on build
Markets
US, UK and Germany
Timeline
About 3 months, PRD to live test

eBay wanted more of its buyers to try selling. The Selling Product team proposed a bigger, personalised "Sell one like this" banner on the listing page, with the copy written by an LLM. With the product manager I wrote the science PRD and scoped the work into a phase we could vet and ship. I designed the system and its evaluation, and built the generation loop myself. About three months later, 1,650 pre-vetted messages went into a three-arm A/B test, with Responsible AI sign-off.

Result. AI copy beat human-written copy on click-through in Germany (statistically significant) and matched it in the US and UK. Neither new panel beat the existing button, and the bigger panel cost buying conversion, so copy iteration stopped.

1 of 4The ask

Growing casual (C2C) selling was a strategic priority. Buyers who also sell tend to be more valuable customers, though that is a correlation, not proof of cause. Research pointed to perception as the barrier: selling feels hard, slow and costly, so many people never start a listing, and many who start never finish.

The idea came from the Selling Product team. They planned to turn the small "Sell one like this" button at the bottom of a listing into a bigger panel, with copy personalised to the buyer. The button opens the listing flow with the item's details pre-filled. Analytics had already built customer segments and a propensity-to-sell model. The team asked my group to use LLMs to write the copy.

Two questions before the build

Why AI? A content team could hand-write three to ten versions in the time available, and each new banner use case took weeks. Writing for 1,650 customer groups needed something else.

Is copy the lever? Probably not the biggest one. Most of the friction sits after the click, in the listing flow. So we named the business north star explicitly, kept the science metric to what copy can move, and put a guardrail on the listing page's main job, which is selling the item on it.

Business north star
New or reactivated listing attempters and listers (NORA and NORL)
Guardrail
Buying conversion on the listing page
Science measure
Click-through rate on the banner
Scale
1,650 customer groups in each of 3 markets

2 of 4Scope and design

The science PRD asked one question: what is the best copy for this buyer, at this moment, within brand, legal, safety and UX rules? Two things were out of scope by design: whether to show the banner at all, and anything after the click.

The disagreement

The Selling team wanted copy personalised to each individual. The product manager and I argued for phasing it, and the case came down to one question.

When the AI makes an error and shows a non-compliant message, are we prepared for the effect?

You can't pre-vet hundreds of millions of one-to-one variants. Rule checks and an LLM judge reduce errors but don't eliminate them. And there was no track record yet for trusting AI-written copy in front of customers. Phasing it won the argument, and the same design is why Responsible AI signed off.

Phase 1: personalise by customer group

Built and tested

  • Copy for pre-defined customer groups
  • Every message generated, evaluated and vetted before launch
  • Listing page first, home page next

Phase 2: one-to-one copy

Deferred

  • Copy generated live for each person
  • Larger pipelines and a stricter Responsible AI and Legal review
  • Earn trust in AI-written copy in Phase 1 first

Customer groups

We could only use segments the serving platform could look up at low latency, so we kept those and dropped the rest. Combined, they make 1,650 customer groups in each market, with one message per group.

There was no evidence on which personalisation hook works for which group, so we let the LLM choose the hook and set the tone by market and expertise: direct in Germany, bold and value-driven in the US, sustainability-led with a little humour in the UK, and simpler for novices.

Plan and teams

About three months from PRD to live test, and an A/B test of about three weeks. Five core teams: Selling Product, Science, Analytics, Design (UX and Content) and Engineering, with Legal on the policy rules and Responsible AI on sign-off.

Humans trained the evaluator; the evaluator trained the generator.

I drafted the evaluation rubric from Content Design's guidelines and refined it with them. Rule checks took the deterministic parts, and a GPT-4 judge took the nuanced ones. Once the judge was settled, I used it to tune the generation prompt and pick the model with the best pass rate. The judge checked compliance only. Whether a message was good was left to the experiment.

The hard part of the online lane was getting offline batch output into a new, locked-down serving platform. I agreed with Data Engineering that they would build the pipeline and handle permissions, latency and service levels, with my team owning it for the test. That split got the test live sooner than building it ourselves.

Evaluation

  • Rule checks for the deterministic: character limits, punctuation, CTA format, message structure
  • LLM judge for the nuanced: legal and value-proposition rules, brand voice and tone, staying on point
  • Rubric drafted from Content's guidelines and refined with Content
  • Prompt tuning against the judge, then the model with the best pass rate

Human in the loop

  • Science-led review, with volunteers from Product, Engineering, Content and Analytics
  • Label Studio: three variants per group, with a written reviewer guide
  • Two tasks: flag issues, and pick the best variant to choose the model and prompt
  • Caught: German tone, punctuation slips and LLM hallucinations

Guardrails

  • No live generation: every message vetted before any customer saw it
  • Responsible AI sign-off, won by that pre-vetting design
  • Pass means compliant; "good" left for the experiment to decide
  • Traceable: every message tagged by hook, so results trace back to the copy

4 of 4Outcome and learnings

The copy held up; the bigger panel didn't. The test ran for about three weeks on the listing page in the US, UK and Germany.

Control (existing button)BaselineBaselineBaselineBaseline
Human copy, new panelNeutralNeutralNeutralDown
AI copy, new panelNeutral, on par with human copyAbove human copy*NeutralDown

* Statistically significant against human copy; neutral against control. Neither new panel beat the existing button on clicks.

Read the rows as two comparisons. Human copy against control isolates the panel: no gain in clicks, and buying conversion dropped. AI copy against human copy isolates the copy: on par in the US and UK, and better in Germany, where the tone guidance was direct. New and reactivated sellers didn't move, which points past the banner to the journey after the click.

Copy iteration stopped because of the placement result.

What went well

  • About three months from PRD to live test, on the committed timeline
  • Five teams aligned, with Legal on policy rules
  • Responsible AI sign-off for customer-facing generated copy
  • Product and Content happy with offline quality
  • In Germany, AI copy beat human copy

Learnings

  • AI is great at generating options; decisions should stay human-led.
  • Human review catches bad copy well; "good" is subjective and better learned from data
  • Tone per market matters: the direct tone won in Germany
  • Banner placement and size can outweigh the copy itself
  • Left to the LLM, the personalisation hook varied from run to run

If I did it again

As science lead

  • Agree a personalisation framework, with many more good examples
  • Rotate variants and read results per hook
  • Design the test to learn the best hook per group, for example a group-level bandit

As product lead

  • Agree decision rules up front, so a placement problem can't end winning copy
  • Test the banner's placement and size on their own, before testing copy
